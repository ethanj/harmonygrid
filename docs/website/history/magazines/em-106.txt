Harmony Grid 
is a Macintosh “software 
instrument” targeted mainly at the musi¬ 
cally untutored—“hobbyists and ama¬ 
teurs who never learned much harmony 
theory, who would learn it if it were 
more fun than practicing scales.” While 
it doesn’t fit easily into existing music 
software categories, the program has at 
least two functions: it’s a tool/toy for the 
exploration of some aspects of harmony 
theory, and it’s a vehicle for live improvi¬ 
sation. 
The program presents musical ele¬ 
ments—scales, modes, and chords—as 
visual patterns on a two-dimensional 
graph (the Harmony Grid) as well as on 
a conventional clavier. You can hear 
these elements by moving the mouse 
over the grid or the clavier, or by playing 
a MIDI keyboard. The tutorial value of 
this approach is that the grid shape of a 
given scale or chord type is uniform 
across all transpositions, as opposed to 
the multiplicity of spatial and fingering

VIEWING AND PLAYING SCALES 
Should the user wish to use the program 
for purposes other than mouse-driven 
improvisation, then learning to read the 
grid is the first order of business. The 
grid can represent the interval pattern 
of a given scale or chord in many differ¬ 
ent ways; the exact way depends on how 
the user calibrates the X-Y axes of the 
grid in units of semitones. For example, 
suppose you want to play with a major 
scale. You first select this scale from the 
ten available types (you can also make 
up and save your own), then set up the 
grid. Fig. 1 shows an A major scale laid 
out on a 2 x 1 grid. Moving from left to 
right you read two semitones per square; 
moving from bottom to top you read 
one semitone per square. The members 
of the scale are circled, with the root of 
the scale heavily outlined. On the cla¬ 
vier, small circles appear on each key 
that is a scale member, again with the 
roots marked by a dark circle. 
The grid’s visual pattern is the same in 
all octaves and remains the same if the 
scale root is changed. The main benefit 
of this particular grid is that it shows the 
whole-step/half-step makeup of the 
scale clearly in terms of location; there¬ 
fore, it is useful for viewing most any tra¬ 
ditional scale or mode. 
Using a MIDI interface and synth, or 
the Mac’s internal sound generator, you 
can play individual notes of the scale in 
real time by clicking on their squares or 
clavier keys, or traverse the scale by 
moving the mouse across the scale notes 
on the grid or clavier; you can also trans¬ 
pose the scale or select a new one. There 
are various other performance options, 
but the main point is that all such im¬ 
provisation is restricted to the given 
scale’s notes. Also, the performance can 
be rhythmically restricted to trigger only 
on ticks of the built-in metronome. This 
recalls the constrained drawing options 
of many paint programs, which allow 
artistic klutzes to draw perfectly straight 
lines. Now musical klutzes can improvise 
in a key without hitting any wrong notes 
or making timing errors. 
VIEWING AND PLAYING CHORDS 
This program isn’t called Scale Grid, so 
where’s the harmony? By clicking on 
one of a group of "buttons,” you can se¬ 
lect one of ten chord types (again, you 
can define and save your own). Suppose 
you select the minor chord type, while 
remaining within the A major scale. By 
moving the mouse across the grid or 
clavier, you find that Harmony Grid at¬ 
tempts to harmonize each scale degree 
by building and playing a partial or 
complete minor triad on that degree. If 
you trigger the notes B, C#, or F#, you 
hear the ii, iii, and vi chords of the scale;
